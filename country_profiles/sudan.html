Aviation System Overview

Sudan's civil aviation system is in a state of near-total collapse following the outbreak of war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in April 2023. Khartoum International Airport (HSSS) — previously the country's primary gateway — has been directly attacked, partially destroyed, and rendered non-operational. Military aircraft and ground equipment have been destroyed on the apron. The airport has not handled scheduled commercial flights since April 2023.

Port Sudan International Airport (HSPN) on the Red Sea coast has become the de facto primary aviation gateway during the conflict period, handling humanitarian flights, diplomatic evacuations, and limited commercial services. Its infrastructure was designed for light regional traffic; the surge of international operations since 2023 has severely strained its capacity, fuel supply, ground handling, and navigation systems. The airport sits at 3m ASL and is affected by the extreme heat of the Red Sea coast — density altitude and dust haze are persistent operational factors.

El Fasher Airport (HSSF) in North Darfur serves as the key aviation node for humanitarian operations in the Darfur region. It has been under persistent threat from RSF operations around El Fasher city, which as of 2025 is the last major Darfur population centre not under RSF control. UN and NGO humanitarian airlifts continue under extreme security constraints.

Sudan Civil Aviation Authority (SCAA) has effectively suspended normal regulatory functions due to the conflict. No meaningful oversight of air operations occurs. Operators must rely on military coordination (SAF for Khartoum-area and Port Sudan; humanitarian coordination bodies for Darfur) for all flight approvals and ground operations.

Top-Line Security Assessment

Sudan is in an active civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), which began in April 2023. The conflict has resulted in the near-destruction of Khartoum as a functional city and has produced the world's largest displacement crisis as of 2024–2025. Aviation operations in Sudan are limited to humanitarian airlift, diplomatic flights, and essential commercial services at Port Sudan — the de facto wartime capital.

Khartoum International Airport (HSSS) was a direct conflict casualty in April 2023, with RSF forces advancing to the airport perimeter, military aircraft destroyed on the apron, and the terminal building sustained structural damage. The airport has not operated since. Do not attempt operations to HSSS under any circumstances without current on-the-ground security verification from armed forces coordination.

Darfur represents a humanitarian catastrophe of historic proportions. El Fasher city and its airport (HSSF) are under a prolonged RSF siege. UN and NGO humanitarian airlifts into HSSF continue but operate in a genuine combat-zone environment. RSF has conducted air attacks on Darfur targets using former SAF aircraft and drone assets. All operations to El Fasher require OCHA humanitarian coordination and UNDSS security clearance.

Port Sudan, while substantially safer than Khartoum or Darfur, has been targeted by drone strikes attributed to RSF. The airport itself was not struck as of early 2026, but the Red Sea coastal region is no longer a genuinely secure rear area. Operators must maintain current security awareness for Port Sudan operations and be prepared for rapid departure contingency if conditions deteriorate.

Active Threat Vectors  [2025–2026]
EXTREME SAF/RSF civil war  ·  Active conflict across Sudan

Conflict between SAF (Sudanese Armed Forces) and RSF (Rapid Support Forces) continues across Sudan. Khartoum remains a conflict zone. Darfur under widespread RSF control with El Fasher besieged. RSF and SAF both possess aerial assets including fast jets, Mi-24 helicopters, and drone systems. Air attacks on civilian infrastructure documented across multiple cities.

Aviation exposure: EXTREME for all operations in conflict zones. Do not operate to Khartoum (HSSS) or RSF-controlled Darfur airports. Port Sudan and El Fasher (humanitarian only) require current security assessment before every movement.

HIGH El Fasher siege  ·  Darfur humanitarian corridor

El Fasher (HSSF) is the only major Darfur city not under RSF control. RSF forces besiege the city and airport access roads are contested. Humanitarian airlifts by WFP, UNHCR, and UN agencies operate under UNDSS security arrangements. Aircraft on ground are exposed to indirect fire and potential direct attack. Approach and departure corridors monitored by both SAF and RSF forces.

Aviation exposure: only operate HSSF under UN/OCHA authorisation; armed escort mandatory all ground movements; maintain departure-ready status at all times; no overnight parking without specific security arrangement.

MODERATE Port Sudan  ·  Drone attack exposure & capacity strain

RSF drone attacks on Port Sudan have been documented in 2025, targeting fuel infrastructure and port facilities. The airport itself was not directly struck but is within the attack radius. Infrastructure capacity is severely strained by the influx of international operations. Fuel availability is inconsistent. Heat and dust create additional operational constraints.

Aviation exposure: confirm fuel 48hrs prior; maintain current security monitoring; have departure contingency planned; extreme heat requires density altitude recalculation in summer months.

MODERATE Extreme environment  ·  Dust, heat & density altitude (HSPN)

Port Sudan sits on the Red Sea coast at 3m ASL. While the low elevation means density altitude is less critical than at highland airports, ambient temperatures of 38–45°C in June–September create a significant heat penalty on performance. Dust and haboob (sandstorm) events reduce visibility to near-zero with very short warning. Haboob season aligns with summer — June through September.

Aviation exposure: performance calculations mandatory for high-temperature periods; monitor dust/haboob warnings; runway surface contamination from sand; aircraft parking exposed to abrasive sand in haboob events.

Seasonal Calendar — Sudan Arid / Semi-Arid Climate
Jun – Sep HABOOB
Summer · Haboob season & extreme heat
Sudan's most operationally challenging weather period. Haboob (massive dust storms driven by convective outflows) can develop with minimal warning, reducing visibility to near-zero over a 10–15 minute onset. Ambient temperatures at Port Sudan reach 42–45°C. Darfur receives localised convective rainfall — flash flooding at HSSF and HSNN. Extreme heat creates significant aircraft performance penalties. This season coincides with the peak of the current conflict, creating a compound operational risk.
Oct – Mar
Cool dry season · Best meteorological conditions
October to March represents the best meteorological window for Sudan aviation. Temperatures moderate, haboob risk minimal, and visibility generally excellent. Northeast trade winds provide stable approach conditions at Port Sudan. Dust haze possible December–January from harmattan influence. The security environment remains unchanged irrespective of season.
Apr – May
Pre-summer transition · Increasing dust activity
Temperature rising rapidly across all of Sudan. Haboob frequency beginning to increase. Darfur region begins receiving early rains. Port Sudan heat reaching 38–40°C. This is the period in which the current civil war began (April 2023), and the security situation remains at its most volatile during political anniversaries.

CRITICAL NOTE — Haboob Avoidance at HSPN
Haboob events at Port Sudan can produce zero visibility and 60kt+ surface winds within 15 minutes of onset with little radar warning. Operators must monitor surface weather reports and satellite imagery, maintain continuous radio contact with HSPN ATC, and have a departure-hold plan for any developing haboob signature. Do not commence approach if haboob reported within 50nm.
