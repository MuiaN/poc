Aviation System Overview

Ethiopia operates one of Africa's largest and most commercially significant aviation systems, dominated by Ethiopian Airlines — the continent's biggest carrier by revenue, fleet size, and network breadth. Addis Ababa Bole International Airport (HAAB) is the hub of this system, located at 2,355m ASL (7,726ft) on the central Ethiopian plateau. This extreme elevation creates a year-round density altitude challenge that is the single most operationally critical physical characteristic of the airport — a matter of fundamental performance calculation for every aircraft type operating in and out of HAAB.

Ethiopian Airlines effectively controls all ground services, catering, and maintenance at HAAB. This vertical integration creates a highly efficient operation for airlines using Ethiopian's services, but limits the independence of external ground handlers and creates dependency on a single provider for most airside services. RFFS at HAAB is Category 10, the highest ICAO level, commensurate with the volume and size of aircraft handled.

Dire Dawa Airport (HADR) is Ethiopia's second international gateway at 1,250m ASL, serving the eastern regions and the Djibouti corridor. It handles a mix of domestic and international traffic but is significantly smaller than HAAB. The Somali border proximity makes HADR operationally sensitive from a security perspective.

Ethiopia's aviation system carries elevated country risk due to the legacy of the Tigray conflict (2020–2022) and ongoing internal security challenges in Amhara, Oromia, and the Somali region. While HAAB itself has remained operationally stable throughout these periods, northern airspace corridors and access to secondary airstrips in conflict-affected regions require careful assessment for each operation.

Top-Line Security Assessment

Ethiopia presents a HIGH overall risk rating for aviation operators, driven by a combination of active internal conflicts, conflict-legacy airspace restrictions, and multi-vector security threats that affect specific corridors and secondary airports. The risk is not uniform: Addis Ababa Bole International (HAAB) is operationally stable and well-managed, but it exists within a country experiencing significant internal security challenges that affect risk loading at the national level.

The Tigray conflict (2020–2022) left a significant security and infrastructure legacy in northern Ethiopia. Mekelle Airport (HAMK) and the wider Tigray air corridor were affected by combat operations, airspace closures, and infrastructure damage. Rehabilitation is ongoing but incomplete as of 2025–2026. Northern corridors require current NOTAM verification before any transit or operation.

The Oromo Liberation Army (OLA) continues armed operations in parts of Oromia region, with periodic clashes with Ethiopian security forces. This affects ground security and access routes in central Ethiopia outside of Addis Ababa. Aviation-specific incidents have not been documented but operational planning to secondary airstrips in Oromia must account for the security environment.

Al-Shabaab maintains a presence along the Somali-Ethiopian border and has demonstrated capability to conduct operations in eastern Ethiopia. HADR (Dire Dawa) and airstrips in the Somali region of Ethiopia are within the zone of elevated threat. No direct aviation-targeting incidents documented at HADR, but ground security protocols are essential.

The primary operational risk at HAAB remains the density altitude environment. At 2,355m ASL, every performance calculation must account for reduced thrust, extended takeoff rolls, reduced climb gradients, and higher approach and landing speeds. In summer months when temperatures reach 25–30°C, density altitude can approach or exceed 10,000ft pressure altitude equivalent.

Active Threat Vectors  [2025–2026]
HIGH Tigray conflict legacy  ·  Northern corridors & HAMK

The Tigray War (2020–2022) resulted in documented attacks on civilian aircraft and airports in the northern region. An Ethiopian Airlines B737 was struck by MANPADS near HAMK on 23 August 2021, forcing an emergency landing. Airspace closures and access restrictions in the Tigray/Amhara corridor were maintained throughout the conflict period.

While the Pretoria Peace Agreement (Nov 2022) ended active hostilities, the security environment in Tigray, Amhara, and Afar regions remains fragile with ongoing armed group activity and political tensions.

Aviation exposure: avoid northern Ethiopia airspace below FL200 without current NOTAM clearance; HAMK operations require pre-clearance and security assessment; overflight routing over Tigray/Amhara should be confirmed at planning stage.

HIGH Density altitude  ·  HAAB performance-critical environment

HAAB at 2,355m / 7,726ft ASL is one of the highest-elevation major international airports in Africa. Summer temperatures of 25–30°C in June–September push density altitude to 9,500–10,500ft equivalent. All aircraft performance calculations must use actual DA, not field elevation. Overweight departures, reduced fuel loads for range, and extended rotation distances are operational consequences.

Aviation exposure: takeoff weight limitations — operators must recalculate for every departure; longer TORA required; reduced obstacle clearance margins; higher Vr and V2 speeds; engine-out procedures affected by terrain and reduced climb capability.

MODERATE OLA / Amhara Fano  ·  Internal insurgency & secondary airports

Oromo Liberation Army (OLA) and Amhara Fano militia have been in conflict with Ethiopian federal forces in Oromia and Amhara regions. Airstrips in these areas carry elevated ground security risk. Direct aviation targeting not documented but armed group presence near airstrips has been reported.

Aviation exposure: secondary strip operations in Oromia/Amhara — pre-arrange security escort; do not rely on standing ground security; assess before every operation; humanitarian operators particularly should verify with NGO security clusters.

MODERATE CB season  ·  Significant convective activity Jun–Sep

Ethiopian highlands experience intense CB activity during the Kiremt (big rains) season June–September. CB tops exceeding FL500 documented around the Ethiopian plateau. Rift Valley storms can develop rapidly. Afternoon operations at HAAB are most affected. The combination of density altitude and CB weather in the same seasonal window (Jun–Sep) represents the highest composite operational risk period.

Aviation exposure: flight planning must account for CB avoidance routing; fuel reserves increased for CB deviations; afternoon departure delays common at HAAB Jun–Sep; density altitude + CB = compound risk window.

Seasonal Calendar — Ethiopian Highland Rainfall
Jun – Sep KIREMT
Kiremt (Big rains) · PRIMARY SEASON · Highest risk
Most intense rainfall season on the Ethiopian highlands. Severe CB activity across the plateau with tops documented above FL500. This season combines maximum convective weather risk with maximum density altitude risk — the two compound to create the highest composite aviation risk window of the year. Afternoon operations particularly affected. HAAB experiences significant delays. Departure weight limitations at maximum.
Oct – Jan
Bega (Dry season) · Best operational window
Cool, dry, stable. Best visibility and approach conditions. Density altitude more manageable due to cooler temperatures. Optimal period for all Ethiopian aviation. HAAB at lowest composite risk. Ethiopian Airlines peak international schedule utilisation. Clear horizons and excellent VMC conditions.
Feb – May
Belg (Small rains) · Secondary season
Southern Ethiopia and Rift Valley receive short rains. Northern plateau relatively dry. Some CB activity in central highland areas but less intense than Kiremt. Temperature rising through this period begins to increase density altitude impact at HAAB. Moderate operational risk.
