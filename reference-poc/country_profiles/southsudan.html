Aviation System Overview

South Sudan's aviation system is defined almost entirely by humanitarian and NGO operations. The country gained independence in 2011 but descended into civil war in December 2013. A fragile peace agreement signed in 2018 has not produced durable stability, and aviation infrastructure remains severely degraded by years of conflict, extreme underfunding, and near-total absence of commercial investment.

Juba International Airport (HSSJ) is the only airport in the country with paved runways and functional IFR instrumentation. It sits at 457m ASL and handles the entirety of South Sudan's commercial and humanitarian air traffic. The airport is operated jointly by civilian and military authorities and has sustained periodic damage from conflict activity. Ground services are extremely limited — fuel availability must be pre-confirmed, and handling capacity can be overwhelmed during UN or NGO surge operations.

Beyond Juba, the country has approximately 80 registered airstrips of varying serviceability. Malakal (HSSM), Wau (HSWW), Bentiu (HSNB), Bor (HSBO), and Rumbek (HSMK) handle WFP and UN Mission in South Sudan (UNMISS) airbridge traffic. Most are unpaved, subject to seasonal flooding, and lack any ground emergency services. Serviceability of these strips is highly time-sensitive and must be confirmed within 24–48 hours of planned operations.

The Nile basin location of Juba creates persistent crosswind and windshear exposure on approach to Runway 03/21, particularly during afternoon convective activity. Armed escort is mandatory for all crew and passenger ground movements outside the airport perimeter. Pre-arrangement of all services — fuel, handling, security — is required a minimum of 48 hours in advance.

Top-Line Security Assessment

South Sudan represents one of the highest-risk operating environments in sub-Saharan Africa. The security environment is characterised by multiple armed factions, persistent intercommunal violence, a legacy of civil war infrastructure destruction, and an absence of effective state authority outside Juba. Aviation assets are neither targeted nor protected in any systematic way — they operate in and around an active conflict zone.

MANPADS weapons captured from SPLA and opposition forces during the 2013–2018 civil war remain unaccounted for. The threat of missile attack on aircraft — particularly on departure and approach at HSSJ — is a credible and documented concern that underpins war risk loadings across the country. No confirmed MANPADS attack on a civil aircraft has occurred, but documented military use of the weapons against aircraft during intra-government clashes is on record.

Intercommunal violence is geographically pervasive across all ten states, driven by cattle raiding, land disputes, and political manipulation of ethnic grievances. The armed groups involved are not structured terrorist organisations but are capable of firing on aircraft perceived as belonging to a rival faction or government force. This has occurred at multiple locations including Malakal, Bor, and Wau.

Armed escorts are mandatory for all personnel movements outside the airport boundary. This is not a precautionary recommendation — it reflects the actual ground threat level in and around all populated centres including Juba. Operations after 1800 local are strongly discouraged at all locations outside HSSJ.

Active Threat Vectors
HIGH MANPADS & small arms  ·  Nationwide

Unaccounted MANPADS inventory from 2013–2018 civil war. Multiple armed factions hold IGLA/SA-7 class weapons. Aircraft on approach and departure from HSSJ, Malakal, Bor, and Wau are within threat envelope. No confirmed civil aircraft strike since 2018 ceasefire, but military aircraft fired on documented in conflict period.

Aviation exposure: all civil and humanitarian aircraft on approach/departure to all South Sudan airports. War risk loadings fully justified. No safe operating altitude corridor below FL100 over contested territory.

HIGH Intercommunal armed violence  ·  All states

Cattle raiding, ethnic conflict, and political militia activity documented in all ten states. SPLA-IO splinter factions active in Equatoria region. LRA legacy presence in Western Equatoria. Jonglei and Unity States remain most active. Armed groups have fired on both military and civilian aircraft in prior incidents at Malakal and Bor.

Aviation exposure: low-level and approach flight paths over armed group territory; ground operations at remote airstrips without perimeter security; helicopter operations in particular.

MODERATE Infrastructure failure  ·  HSSJ and upcountry

ATC communications unreliable after 2200L at HSSJ. Navigation aids subject to power outages. Fuel supply chains disrupted by poor road infrastructure and periodic armed theft of fuel convoys. Remote airstrip surfaces are often degraded by seasonal flooding with no systematic maintenance regime. Emergency response capability at HSSJ is well below ICAO Annex 14 standards.

Aviation exposure: night operations inadvisable; IFR operations unreliable; fuel planning must include full mission reserves with no expectation of uplifting fuel at destination.

MODERATE Political instability  ·  Juba-centric

Revitalised Agreement on Resolution of the Conflict in South Sudan (R-ARCSS) remains fragile. Unification of armed forces incomplete. Upcoming elections repeatedly delayed. Political tension has previously produced sudden closures of airspace and airport access checkpoints. Airport authority structure is subject to inter-agency disputes between SSCA, military, and NSS (National Security Service).

Aviation exposure: unpredictable airport access — checkpoint harassment documented; aircraft on ground subject to inspection by multiple agencies; departure clearances can be withheld without notice.

Seasonal Calendar — South Sudan Single Wet Season
Dec – Mar
Dry season · Optimal operational period
Harmattan influence from north reduces humidity and convective activity. HSSJ visibility excellent. Remote airstrips generally dry and serviceable. Dust haze occasional Dec–Jan, reducing visibility to 3,000–5,000m on worst days but rarely operationally limiting. Best period for upcountry strip operations. Grass airstrips firm.
Apr – May TRANSITION
Onset of rains · Increasing convective risk
ITCZ advances northward. Isolated heavy thunderstorms begin appearing, particularly afternoons. Nile corridor crosswinds intensify. Remote strips beginning to soften. Pre-flight serviceability checks for upcountry destinations become critical from April onwards.
Jun – Oct HIGH RISK
Wet season · Severe operational disruption
South Sudan's single extended wet season drives widespread flooding. Sudd wetland expands dramatically. Remote airstrips in Upper Nile, Unity, and Jonglei states become waterlogged and impassable for weeks at a time. Juba less affected but afternoon CB activity requires flexible departure planning. LLWS on Rwy 03/21 approach during squall line passage. Visibility frequently below 1,500m in heavy rain. WFP and UNMISS airlift operations operating at surge capacity.
Nov – Dec
Late rains / Early dry · Gradual improvement
Convective activity diminishing but residual flooding persists in low-lying areas. Sudd drainage takes 4–6 weeks after last rains. Airstrip serviceability improving but still requires individual pre-confirmation. Dust haze begins returning Nov from the Sahel.

Underwriter Context

South Sudan represents the most challenging underwriting environment in the East Africa portfolio. The combination of active conflict exposure, infrastructure failure, and extreme weather creates a compound risk profile that is not adequately reflected by incident records alone — the absence of reporting infrastructure means significant under-counting of events.

War risk loadings are fully justified for all operations in South Sudan. Hull risk for operations at upcountry strips should reflect the high probability of surface-related damage, the near-total absence of RFFS, and the impossibility of recovery operations in many locations. Cargo coverage should reflect documented theft and diversion of humanitarian supplies.

The UNMISS aviation safety framework represents the best available intelligence on airstrip serviceability and security conditions. Operators outside the UN system should maintain formal liaison with UNMISS Air Operations prior to any upcountry mission.
